How to Find a Hidden Water Leak in Your House

A rising water bill, a spinning meter with everything off and a musty smell are the common signs. A simple meter test narrows it down.

By the Norton Water Damage Pros team · Updated November 12, 2026
24/7 Emergency Dispatch
(508) 286-8971
☎ Call Now

Hidden leaks do their damage out of sight. By the time a stain shows, the cavity may be saturated. This guide covers checks a homeowner can do in an afternoon, from the meter test to a toilet dye test, and the point at which a plumber or a thermal camera is worth calling.

If you find active water, shut off the main and call. Do not wait to find the exact spot.

Start with the water meter

Turn off every fixture and appliance that uses water, including ice makers and irrigation. Find the water meter, note the reading or watch the small leak indicator. Wait fifteen minutes without using any water and check again. If the meter moved, you have a leak somewhere between the meter and the fixtures.

Close the main valve in the house and check the meter again. If it still moves, the leak is between the meter and the house. If it stops, the leak is inside. Write down what you find, because a plumber will ask.

Where to look next

After the meter test, walk the house with a flashlight.

Hidden leak checklist

  • Look under every sink for damp cabinet bases and drips
  • Check behind and under the washer, dishwasher and refrigerator
  • Inspect the water heater and its pan
  • Look at ceilings below bathrooms for stains
  • Listen at walls for running water
  • Add food coloring to the toilet tank and wait 15 minutes
  • Check the basement or crawl space for wet joists and drips

A leak you find is a leak you can fix. A leak you cannot find may be in a wall or under a slab.

Using tools: meters and cameras

A moisture meter checks walls and floors for hidden moisture. A thermal camera shows cooler wet areas. A professional leak detection service may use acoustic microphones to hear a leak in a pipe, or tracer gas for hard cases.

A restoration crew can map the wet area with meters and a camera. A plumber finds and repairs the pipe. The two work in sequence.

Common hidden sources

Supply lines to toilets and sinks, ice-maker lines, washing machine hoses, a dripping water heater, a failing shower pan, an irrigation line, a roof flashing and a condensate drain are common. In an older home, galvanized or copper lines can corrode through.

Frozen pipes cause hidden leaks too when they split and thaw. See frozen pipe water damage for the pattern.

What to do once you find it

Shut the water off at the nearest valve. Photograph the leak and the damage. Call a plumber to repair it. If there is water damage, call a restoration crew to dry the area. Starting water extraction and drying quickly limits the spread.

Keep the plumber's invoice. It documents the repair and helps with any claim.

Preventing the next one

Know where your main shutoff is and exercise it once a year. Replace old rubber hoses with braided ones. Install leak sensors near appliances and the water heater. Check your water bill monthly for jumps.

A smart water monitor on the main can detect unusual flow and shut off the supply. Ask a plumber whether one suits your home.

Outdoor and irrigation leaks

A leak does not have to be inside the house. A cracked hose bib, a broken irrigation line or a damaged service line between the meter and the house can waste large amounts of water. Signs include soggy ground, unusually green patches in dry weather, a sinking spot in the lawn and the sound of water near the meter.

Walk the yard after a dry spell. If one area stays wet or green, mark it. A plumber or a leak detection service can locate a buried line without digging up the whole yard.

Reading your water bill

Compare this month's usage with the same month last year. A sudden rise with no change in household habits suggests a leak. Some utilities offer online usage graphs by day or hour. A flat overnight usage line, when everyone is asleep and nothing is running, points to a constant leak.

If you use a private well, listen for the pump cycling on and off when nothing is running. A pump that kicks on every few minutes suggests water is escaping somewhere in the system.

Why a quick fix is worth it

Hidden leaks cause the most expensive water damage because they go unnoticed. A pinhole in a copper line can soak insulation, drywall and framing over months. Finding it early turns a large job into a small plumbing repair.

Check the usual suspects once a season and write the date on your calendar. Five minutes under the sinks and behind the washer is cheap protection.

What a professional adds

A plumber with acoustic equipment can hear the sound of water escaping from a pressurized pipe. Infrared cameras show temperature differences caused by leaking hot water. Pressure tests isolate a section of pipe. Video cameras inspect drain lines.

If your own checks point to a leak but you cannot find it, one of these tools usually can. The cost is usually modest compared with opening walls blindly.

Key points to remember

Begin with the water meter test, then walk the house with a flashlight and a moisture meter. Check bills for jumps, listen for running water and look for stains, smells and warm floor spots. Shut off the water when you find an active leak and call a plumber for the repair. Call a restoration crew to dry the area. A small leak found early costs far less than a large one found late.

A Norton note on the water supply

Not every Norton home uses the same water source. Some use town water and others use private wells. Ask the Norton building department or water department which applies to your address. If you use a well, a constantly running pump is a clue to a leak. A town water customer can compare bills against past usage.
