Mold Clearance Testing for a Home Sale or Loan

Clearance testing checks that remediation worked. It should come from an independent party, and it works best alongside moisture records.

By the Norton Water Damage Pros team · Updated November 12, 2026
24/7 Emergency Dispatch
(508) 286-8971
☎ Call Now

Buyers and lenders sometimes ask for proof that a mold problem has been dealt with. That proof can include a written scope, photographs, moisture readings and sometimes testing. This article explains what clearance testing is and what to ask for. It is general information and does not give legal or medical advice.

Any home sale has its own paperwork. Ask your agent or attorney what applies to yours.

What clearance testing is

Clearance testing takes place after remediation to check that the area is ready for rebuilding or occupancy. It can include a visual inspection for remaining growth and moisture, and sometimes air or surface samples. A lab reports the results.

It does not diagnose health effects and does not guarantee that mold will not return. It documents the condition of the area on a given day.

Independent versus the remediation company

The party that tests should be independent of the party that did the work. A company that grades its own work has an incentive to pass it. An independent assessor writes the scope, may inspect during the work and then clears the area.

Ask whether the assessor follows a published protocol, such as the approach in ANSI/IICRC S520, and what the criteria for clearance are before sampling begins.

Limits of testing

Air samples are a snapshot. Spore counts vary with weather, activity and time of day. A single test can pass or fail by chance. Surface samples tell you what is on a spot, not what is in the air.

For that reason, testing works best alongside visual inspection and moisture data. A clear, dry structure with a documented source repair is the stronger evidence.

Timing in a sale

If mold turns up during an inspection, the sale may pause while the parties decide who pays for remediation. Get the scope in writing, get quotes and arrange clearance. Keep the timeline realistic, since drying, removal and rebuild take days.

If you are the seller, collecting records in advance can save time. If you are the buyer, ask what water events the home has had and what was done.

Choosing an assessor

Ask about training and the standards the assessor works to, how samples are taken, which lab is used and what the report includes. Ask for a sample report. Check whether your state licenses mold assessors, and ask the assessor to confirm.

Avoid anyone who sells both testing and removal on the same job without disclosing it.

What a sampling plan looks like

A written sampling plan states how many samples will be taken, where, and how they will be compared. Indoor samples are usually compared with an outdoor sample taken the same day, because outdoor air is the baseline for spore counts. The plan names the lab and the type of analysis.

Ask for the plan before testing day. A plan written after the results come in is not a plan. If the assessor will not share it in advance, consider a different assessor.

Reading a clearance report

A good report lists the date, the weather, the locations sampled, the method, the lab results and a plain statement of whether the area met the criteria set in the plan. It also notes visual findings, such as remaining staining, and moisture readings.

Read the summary and the notes. If the report says the area passed but notes elevated moisture in a wall, ask what that means for the rebuild. The numbers matter, and so do the notes beside them.

Disclosures and the paper trail

Rules about what a seller must disclose vary and are not set by a restoration company. Ask your real estate attorney or agent what applies to your sale. In general, keeping honest records of water events and repairs protects both sides.

A folder with the scope of work, photos, moisture readings, invoices and any clearance report is the best answer to a buyer's question. It shows what happened and what was done.

If a test comes back high

A high result does not always mean the work failed. It may mean the area was disturbed during sampling, the outdoor baseline was unusually low or a source of moisture remains. The assessor and the remediation company review the cause, and they may re-clean, dry further and test again.

Do not rebuild over an area that has not cleared. Closing a wall over damp or contaminated material starts the problem again. Keep communication with the assessor and the contractor clear and in writing.

Cost, timing and who to hire

Testing is usually a small part of the cost of a remediation project, and independent assessors typically schedule within days. Ask for a written quote that lists the number of samples and the report format. Ask how long the lab takes to return results, because the closing timeline may depend on it.

If a lender or buyer asked for the test, send them the plan in advance so they accept the method. That avoids paying twice.

Key points to remember

Clearance testing documents conditions after remediation. Use an independent assessor, agree the plan in advance and read the whole report, including moisture notes. Combine testing with a written scope, photographs and dry readings. Ask your attorney or agent about disclosure rules for your sale. Remember that no test promises that mold will not return, and the strongest protection is a dry building.

A Norton note on records and permits

If remediation involves rebuilding walls, permit rules vary, so ask the Norton building department. For flood exposure questions that a buyer may raise, point to the FEMA Flood Map Service Center, and note that the Town of Norton's 2018 resilience workshop summary names flooded roads including Richardson Avenue.

A short checklist for sellers

Gather the water event timeline, plumber and roofer invoices, moisture readings, remediation scope, photos and any clearance report. Put them in one folder. Offer them early. Documentation turns a vague worry into a documented repair, and it saves time at the table.

  • Keep the work area closed until testing is done
