Mold Removal From a Basement: What the Process Involves

Removing mold from a basement is as much about the water as the mold. Fixing the moisture comes first, then containment, removal and cleaning.

By the Norton Water Damage Pros team · Updated November 12, 2026
24/7 Emergency Dispatch
(508) 286-8971
☎ Call Now

A basement holds moisture better than any other room. Concrete wicks groundwater, wall cavities trap humidity and stored cardboard feeds growth. When mold shows up, the question becomes how a professional would remove it. This article explains the usual stages, based on the published S520 standard for mold remediation, without making any health claims.

For health questions about mold exposure, talk to your physician. This page is about materials and process.

Step one is always water

The EPA states that the key to mold control is moisture control. If the water source remains, any cleaning is temporary. A crew starts by finding where the water comes from: a leak in a wall, groundwater through the slab, poor drainage outside or condensation on cold surfaces.

Only after the source is addressed does remediation make sense. Drying uses dehumidifiers and air movers, as described under basement water damage cleanup.

The remediation sequence

The stages below follow the general approach described in ANSI/IICRC S520.

1

Assess

A visual inspection and moisture mapping define the affected area.

2

Contain

Plastic sheeting seals the area and a negative air machine with HEPA filtration keeps spores from moving.

3

Remove

Porous materials with growth, such as drywall and insulation, are bagged and removed.

4

Clean and dry

Remaining surfaces are cleaned, HEPA vacuumed and dried to normal readings.

After cleaning, the area is checked before rebuilding starts.

What is cleaned and what is removed

Material decides the method.

MaterialTypical approachReason
Concrete and blockCleaned and driedNon-porous or semi-porous, can be cleaned
Wood framingCleaned, sometimes sandedSolid wood can be cleaned
Drywall and insulationRemovedPorous, growth penetrates
Stored cardboard and fabricDiscardedPorous and hard to clean

The mold remediation page describes the full process and the questions to ask any company.

Why bleach alone is not the answer

Bleach whitens a stain and does not penetrate porous materials. It also does nothing about the moisture. Painting over mold hides it while it keeps growing underneath.

The more reliable approach is to remove growth from porous materials, clean the rest and dry the structure so there is nothing to grow on.

What containment looks like in a basement

Containment starts with plastic sheeting over doorways and openings, sealed with tape. A zipper door lets the crew pass without breaking the seal. A negative air machine pulls air from the work area through a HEPA filter and exhausts it outside, so the area stays at lower pressure than the rest of the house.

Floor protection and a decontamination area at the exit help keep dust from traveling. In a basement with stairs, the crew usually seals the top of the stairs. The aim is simple: keep what is disturbed inside the work area.

What happens to removed materials

Contaminated drywall, insulation and cardboard are wrapped or bagged before they leave the work area so that dust does not spread. They go out in the house through a protected route and into a disposal container. Ask the company how it handles disposal.

Ask what will be photographed before and after. A record of the removed areas helps you, your insurer and any buyer who asks later.

After removal: drying, checking and rebuilding

Cleaning uses HEPA vacuums and damp wiping. The area is dried to normal readings, and the crew checks for remaining moisture. Only then does rebuilding begin with new insulation, drywall and paint.

Some owners ask for clearance testing after the work, particularly before a sale or loan. That subject has its own article. Whatever you choose, keep the written scope, the photos and the final readings.

Questions about licensing and standards

Ask whether your state licenses mold work and whether the company holds any required licence. Ask whether its crews work to ANSI/IICRC S520, the published standard for mold remediation. A standard does not guarantee quality, but a company that follows one can show you how.

Ask for a written scope that lists the area, the containment, the materials to be removed and the verification at the end. A clear scope helps if a buyer, lender or insurer asks what was done.

A realistic timeline for a basement job

Many basement remediation jobs take a few days for containment, removal and cleaning, plus additional days for drying and rebuild. Larger losses take longer. The job rarely begins the day you call, because the moisture source must be found and fixed first.

Plan for a period when part of the basement is closed. Stored items will need to be moved, and the crew may ask for access to the stairs and the exterior. Clear communication saves time.

Preventing a repeat in the same basement

Prevention is mostly about water. Keep gutters clear and downspouts discharging at least several feet from the foundation. Grade soil away from the walls. Test the sump pump each season and consider a battery backup. Insulate cold water pipes that sweat in summer.

Run a dehumidifier through humid months and watch the hygrometer. Replace carpet in a damp basement with materials that tolerate moisture, such as sealed concrete or tile. Inspect stored items twice a year.

Documenting the work for a buyer or lender

If you plan to sell, keep the scope of work, photos before and after, the final moisture readings and any clearance report. A buyer's inspector may ask for them. A clear paper trail turns a worry into a documented repair.

What the homeowner can expect day to day

Expect a visit to assess, a visit to set containment, one or more days of removal and cleaning, and drying visits afterwards. Noise from the air filtration machine is constant. Foot traffic in the work area stops while containment is up.

What Norton records say about wet ground

Norton's 2018 Municipal Vulnerability Preparedness workshop summary describes much of the town as low-lying, with flooded roads at Richardson Avenue, Walker Street and Route 123. Low ground means higher groundwater in wet seasons, which is the situation basements face. Check the FEMA flood map for your address and ask the Norton building department about drainage.

Choosing who does the work

Ask whether crews work to S520, how the area will be contained, what happens to removed materials and what the scope includes. Ask whether your state licenses mold work. Companies in Mansfield, Plainville and Norton can answer the same questions.

What to do first, and what not to do

These cautions apply before and during any basement mold job.

Safety steps

  • Stop the water first
  • Do not disturb growth without containment
  • Do not mix cleaning products
  • Keep children and pets out of the work area
