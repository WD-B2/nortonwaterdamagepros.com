When standing water is an emergency

Ankle-deep water in a basement is an emergency. So is an inch of water across a kitchen that has soaked into the cabinets. Water that reaches electrical outlets, or water that came from a toilet or drain, is also an emergency, and the second case needs a crew with protective gear.

Norton gives homeowners plenty of reasons to expect this call. The town's 2018 resilience workshop summary described much of Norton as low-lying, with flooded roads at Richardson Avenue, Walker Street and Route 123. If your street sits near one of those low spots, a basement that takes water in a hard rain should be pumped the same day, not the next weekend.

What happens on an extraction call

Water extraction has one job, which is to get liquid water out before it becomes moisture in the structure. The sequence below is the same whether the water is two inches deep in a den or six inches deep in a basement.

1

Find the source

The crew confirms the water has stopped or shuts it off, and checks the panel and outlets before anyone steps in.

2

Place the pump

Submersible pumps go into the deepest point and hoses run to a safe discharge.

3

Extract the rest

Extraction units and wands pull water out of carpet, pad and low spots the pump cannot reach.

4

Hand off to drying

Moisture meters map what is still wet, and drying equipment takes over the damp materials.

Extraction is the first stage, not the whole job. Wet drywall, pad and subfloor still hold moisture after the standing water is gone, which is why a crew moves straight into structural drying and dehumidification once the floor is clear.

Pumps, wands and units: the equipment, in plain words

A submersible pump moves deep water fast, but it stops working when the water gets shallow. That is where weighted extraction wands and portable extraction units take over, because they pull water out of carpet fibers and from the thin film across a slab that a pump cannot touch.

Crews also carry moisture meters and a thermal imaging camera. The meter gives a number for each material, and the camera shows cooler, wetter areas behind a finished wall. Those readings are the record of what was wet on day one, and they are what makes the later drying measurable.
