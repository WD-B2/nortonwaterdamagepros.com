Hardwood Floor Water Damage in Norton, MA

Wood takes on water slowly and gives it up slowly. Drying a hardwood floor quickly can save boards that would otherwise cup or buckle.

Hardwood floors swell as they absorb water, and the boards can cup, crown or buckle. The sooner moisture is pulled out, the more likely the floor can be saved. After a leak, a crew measures the wood and the subfloor, then decides whether to dry in place or replace.

If water is on the floor, mop up what you can, lift furniture off the surface and photograph the damage. Do not use heat to speed drying, since it can crack the boards. Call with questions and a crew can check readings.

What water does to a wood floor

Water enters through gaps between boards and soaks into the wood, which swells across its width. Boards pushed against each other lift at the edges, a pattern called cupping, or push upward at the center, called crowning. In bad cases they buckle away from the subfloor.

Finish can hide the damage at first. Dark stains, white clouding, and a musty smell point to moisture sitting under the boards. A meter is the only reliable way to know what is wet below the surface.

Why hardwood moves so much after a leak

Wood takes in water along its width more than its length, so boards grow wider and push against each other. A floor that was nailed or glued tightly has nowhere to go, and the boards lift or cup. The reverse happens during drying: boards shrink back, often leaving gaps.

Floors installed over a basement or a crawl space are exposed to moisture from below. A leak in the space beneath can damage a floor that has never had water on top of it, which is one reason the underside of the floor is checked as well as the surface.

Drying mats, tenting and holes in the subfloor

Specialty drying systems can pull water out through the top of the boards. A mat is laid on the floor, sealed and connected to a pump so it draws moisture up. In some cases small holes are drilled in the toe-kick or baseboard to move air behind the boards.

These methods do not suit every floor. Glued-down floors, very wide boards and floors with a thick finish respond differently, and a crew chooses based on readings taken on the first visit.

Sanding, refinishing and matching

After a floor dries and settles, light cupping often can be sanded flat and the finish renewed. Matching stain on a partly replaced floor is hard, so some owners choose to refinish the whole room.

Ask for samples before you decide. Daylight changes the color of stain, and a spot test in a closet or under a rug shows how a patch will look once the floor is finished.
