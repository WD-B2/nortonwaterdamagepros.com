Chartley is one of Norton's historic villages, near the Attleboro line and along Route 123. It is a short drive from Norton Center, so a crew heading there starts from the same base as any other Norton call.

Most Chartley calls begin the same way. Someone finds water on a floor, a wet ceiling or a basement with an inch or more, and wants to know what to do first. The dispatcher asks where the water came from and whether it is still running, then explains the first steps while the crew is on the way.

Where Chartley is

Wikipedia-based reference entries describe Chartley as a village near the Attleboro line, with its own post office, Route 123 running through the area and Chartley Pond, which formed from iron excavations in the 17th and 18th centuries. A train station served the village in the early and mid 1900s. We use those landmarks to place the village, because there is no published boundary.

If your street runs toward the pond or the Attleboro line, you are in the area this page describes. Neighboring villages include Barrowsville, which has its own page.

Ponds, dams and drainage in the town report

Norton's Community Resilience Building report, posted on Mass.gov, lists the dams at Barrowsville and Chartley as being evaluated for removal or restoration, describing the Chartley dam as repaired. It also says the stormwater systems around Winnecunnet and Chartley are vulnerable.

That is a general planning finding. It does not say a given home in Chartley has flooded, and we do not claim one has. What it does mean is that anyone near the pond or a low road should check the FEMA flood map for their address and ask the Norton building department about local rules.

If Water Is Still Spreading in Chartley: Prepare Before the Crew Arrives

These steps take minutes and need no tools. They protect people first and the claim record second.

Stop the source if it is safe

Close the main or fixture valve only if you can reach it without standing in water near electrical equipment.

Photograph first

Take wide and close photos of every wet room before moving anything.

Lift what you can

Move papers, electronics and small furniture to dry, higher spots.

Give the crew a path

Clear a route from the door to the wet area and note where a truck can park.

If a toilet or drain backed up, keep people and pets out of the room and do not use ordinary fans or a household vacuum.

Documenting the loss for your insurer

Before cleanup begins, photograph the water, the source and any affected belongings. Keep receipts and write down when you noticed it. A restoration crew can add moisture readings and a written scope of work to that record.

None of this guarantees coverage. Ask your insurer what your policy covers and whether flood or sewer backup is included.

Extraction first, then drying

Standing water comes out with pumps and extraction units. Afterward, air movers and dehumidifiers pull moisture from drywall, framing and subfloors, and readings are taken until the materials reach a dry standard. The work follows ANSI/IICRC S500, which describes water damage restoration.

If a home in Chartley is on a septic system and the water came from a drain, a septic professional may be part of the answer. See sewage backup cleanup for how contaminated water is handled.
