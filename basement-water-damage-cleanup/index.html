A wet basement is an emergency when water is standing or still coming in. Emergency basement water damage calls in Norton go to our 24/7 line, and a crew can be dispatched the same day when available. The first questions are how deep the water is, where it is entering and whether the sump pump is running.

If the sump pump has failed, do not wade in until power is off. Move items off the floor if they are dry, and keep children and pets away. Photograph the water level and the entry point.

Basement water in a low-lying town

Norton's own resilience planning points to the same pressure homeowners feel. The town's 2018 workshop summary described much of Norton as low-lying, with flooded roads at Richardson Avenue, Walker Street and Route 123. Low ground tends to hold water longer, which is hard on basements.

Check the FEMA flood map for your address, and ask the Norton building department about drainage and any local rules before you add drains or a pump. Permit rules vary, so ask before you start. The village page for Chartley adds local drainage notes.

Where basement water comes from

Water enters through the floor-wall joint, cracks in the slab, window wells, a failed sump or a pipe above. Each source has a different pattern, and knowing it tells a crew what to dry.

Groundwater seepage tends to start at the edges and rise, a plumbing leak starts under the pipe and spreads, and a window well overflow runs in under the sill. A moisture map on the first visit separates these before drying begins.

Sump pumps, backup power and prevention

A sump pump moves groundwater out of a pit in the slab. It needs power, so a storm that knocks out electricity can stop it just when it is needed. Battery backups and water-powered backups exist, and a plumber can advise which fits your system.

Test the pump before wet seasons by pouring water into the pit and watching it start. Clear gutters, extend downspouts and check the grading around the foundation, since outside water is easier to stop than inside water.
